## Findings

Surveys in 2010–2011 compared 310 sites across seven land-use classes in Santarém and Paragominas, eastern Amazonia. The study combined these observational comparisons with land-cover transition rates to estimate impacts for 18 transitions. Logged-and-burned primary forest had 25% lower large-tree richness and 27% lower dung-beetle richness than undisturbed primary forest. Aboveground carbon was 28–46% lower in logged or logged-and-burned forest than in undisturbed forest. Forest conversion to pasture was both prevalent and high impact when ecological differences were combined with transition rates.

These findings show why forest area alone is an incomplete measure of ecological condition: biodiversity and carbon also differ among forests that remain standing.

## Using these data

The table contains site-level richness for large trees, small trees, lianas, birds, ants, dung beetles and orchid bees, plus aboveground, dead-wood, litter and soil carbon. Forest-species richness is supplied separately. Use the site aggregates to compare biodiversity and carbon among land-use classes, including degraded and regenerating forests. The paper's transition-effect estimates, uncertainty and regional transition frequencies require its analyses and additional inputs.

## Limits

- These are comparisons among sites, not repeated measurements before and after interventions. Results from the two study regions are not causal estimates applicable uniformly across Amazonia.
- Missing values remain NA; orchid bees were sampled only in Paragominas. Forest species means species recorded at least once at a primary-forest site, not necessarily forest specialists.
- Carbon is expressed in Mg C per hectare, with soil carbon measured at 0–30 cm depth. Coordinates require a verified join and CRS before mapping.
