# Amazon fisheries: harvest trends and resilience

## Question

Can changes in catch composition reveal declining resilience in multispecies fisheries when total catches do not clearly decline?

## What the study found

Across six Amazon landings series, landed biomass varied without a consistent downward trend, while mean body size declined and species composition changed. Evenness trends differed by region. In the theoretical food-web models, compensating species initially maintained harvests; continued depletion reduced compensation, and communities with fewer species reached collapse sooner under the modeled harvest targets.

## How to use the evidence

Total landed biomass can conceal changes in the species supporting a fishery. The authors interpret composition, body-size and evenness patterns as warning signs consistent with exploitation and model-derived loss of resilience. Delayed collapse with higher biodiversity is a theoretical result, not an observed effect of a conservation intervention or a causal estimate from these regional tables.

## How the evidence was obtained

Observational landings analysis interpreted with theoretical food-web models

## What this dataset contributes

Annual regional summary metrics from the observed landings series; not model trajectories or stock assessments.

## Limits and interpretation

- Landings omit fishing effort and subsistence harvests. Market catches are not a complete census of fish communities, and other pressures can affect composition.
- The theoretical results depend on harvest targets, community-level exploitation and modeled species interactions. Regional evenness trajectories are not interchangeable.
- The prepared table contains annual summaries rather than taxon-level catches, model outputs or region geometries; the periods differ among regions.
