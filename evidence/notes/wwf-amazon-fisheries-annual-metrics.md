## Findings

Across six Amazon landing series, total landed biomass showed no consistent downward trend, while mean body size declined and species composition changed. Evenness trends differed among regions. In the study's theoretical food-web models, catches of other species initially compensated for declines. Continued depletion reduced that compensation, and communities with fewer species reached collapse sooner under the modeled harvest targets.

The authors interpret the observed changes in composition, body size and evenness as warning signs consistent with exploitation and the modeled loss of resilience. Delayed collapse with higher biodiversity is a theoretical result, not an observed effect of conservation or a causal estimate from the landing records.

## Using these data

The table contains 121 annual regional summaries from 1984–2016, with coverage differing among the six regions. Compare changes in landed biomass with changes in body size, species composition and evenness to examine what total catch alone can conceal. These are summaries of observed landings; taxon-level catches, theoretical model trajectories, stock assessments and region geometries are not supplied.

## Limits

- Landings omit fishing effort and subsistence harvests. Market catches are not a complete census of fish communities, and other pressures can affect composition.
- The theoretical results depend on harvest targets, community-level exploitation and modeled species interactions.
- Regional evenness trajectories and observation periods differ; they should not be treated as interchangeable.
