# Catalog summaries

Public descriptions for the prepared collection. Paper findings and the role of each supplied file are distinguished below.

## Amazon dams and waterfalls used to assess fish access to suitable habitat

**Evidence:** Species-distribution and river-connectivity modeling. **Dataset role:** Barrier-location inputs to the study.

Dams can block fish from reaching suitable habitat as the climate changes. A study combining fish-distribution models with river connectivity found that the Amazon–Solimões corridor retains extensive connected, suitable habitat, while planned hydropower expansion and climate change could reduce such areas. This layer provides the dam and waterfall locations used to examine those risks and supports inspection of barriers relevant to energy and river-conservation planning. It contains current barriers and additional future-scenario records. The points are study inputs: the modeled habitat-access results and dam-impact rankings are not included in this layer.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1111/gcb.70685](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.70685).

## Amazon fisheries and the connected wetlands that support them

**Evidence:** Historical catch synthesis and observational spatial analysis. **Dataset role:** Mapped catch summaries underlying a management framework.

Amazon commercial fisheries depend on fish moving between rivers and wetlands. The study found that migratory species accounted for more than 80% of known maximum commercial catches, and linked regional catches of migratory characiform fishes to flooded forest area. These findings support managing fisheries and wetland connections across river basins and national borders. This layer maps historical maximum annual catches for 20 commercial fish taxa across 198 sub-basins and mainstem sections. Values sum maxima from different cities and years; they provide historical context for the study’s management framework, rather than current fish stocks or an estimate of a conservation policy’s effect.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1111/faf.12328](https://doi.org/10.1111/faf.12328).

## Amazon fisheries: harvest trends and resilience

**Evidence:** Observational landings analysis interpreted with theoretical food-web models. **Dataset role:** Annual regional summary metrics from the observed landings series; not model trajectories or stock assessments..

Amazon fish landings shifted toward smaller-bodied species across six regional time series, even though total landed biomass showed no consistent decline. The study combines these observations with food-web models in which species can temporarily replace declining catches, while continued depletion reduces that buffering capacity. The models suggest that higher biodiversity can delay fishery collapse; the landings alone do not demonstrate that outcome. This table provides 121 annual regional records from 1984–2016, including landings, diversity and biomass-weighted traits. It supports scrutiny of warning signs beyond total catch, but lacks fishing effort, subsistence harvests and independent stock surveys needed for a complete fishery assessment.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1098/rspb.2022.0726](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2022.0726).

## Eastern Amazon: biodiversity and carbon across land uses

**Evidence:** Observational site comparisons combined with land-cover transition analysis. **Dataset role:** Site-level biodiversity and carbon inputs underlying comparisons among land-use classes..

Land-use comparisons at 310 eastern Amazon sites show that ecological losses extend beyond complete forest clearance. Logged-and-burned primary forest had 25% lower large-tree richness and 27% lower dung-beetle richness than undisturbed primary forest; aboveground carbon also differed substantially among forest conditions. These are observational comparisons used to estimate transition impacts, not repeated measurements of experimentally changed sites. The supplied table contains site-level richness for seven groups and four carbon pools, with forest-species richness linked separately. It supports examining which ecological components differ across land uses and why tracking forest area alone can miss degradation; it does not itself contain the paper’s estimated transition effects.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1073/pnas.2202310119](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2202310119).

## Barriers to terrestrial connectivity across the Amazon

**Evidence:** Spatial connectivity modeling and area comparison. **Dataset role:** Terrestrial resistance input to the connectivity analysis..

The study examined how dams, forest loss, fire, extraction and roads interrupt ecological connections across the Amazon, and how modeled connectivity differs inside and outside Indigenous Territories and Protected Areas. It found higher connectivity inside those areas. This file is the terrestrial resistance surface used as an input to that analysis: it combines weighted human pressures with natural barriers such as rivers and wetlands. Higher scores mean stronger barriers; supplied values range from 1 to 34. Use it to examine the assumed pattern of terrestrial barriers. It is neither the final connectivity result nor a causal estimate of protection or restoration benefits.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1073/pnas.2418189122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2418189122).

## Tree density across the original Amazon forest

**Evidence:** Forest-inventory baseline with spatial interpolation. **Dataset role:** Interpolated tree-density baseline, trees per hectare..

The study mapped how tree density and diversity vary across Amazonian forest types. It found the highest tree densities in northwestern Amazonia and lower densities in central-southern and eastern areas; its density model explained 43% of variation among inventory plots. This file is the interpolated 0.1-degree density baseline, in trees per hectare, from old-growth inventories of woody individuals at least 10 cm in diameter at breast height. It describes the original lowland forest extent and can provide ecological context for conservation planning. Estimates retain substantial unexplained variation and do not measure current forest loss or restoration success. Map display awaits CRS confirmation.

Availability: Download only: CRS confirmation needed.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6).

## Amazon trees: animal seed dispersal and seed traits

**Evidence:** Species-trait and dispersal-mode compilation. **Dataset role:** Selected species-level dispersal assignments and seed traits from the broader network dataset..

Animal seed dispersal is widespread among Amazonian trees: the study’s two classifications assign roughly four in five sampled species to animal-mediated dispersal. This highlights a potential dependence of forest regeneration on animals, rather than demonstrating a restoration response to protecting them. The supplied table contains dispersal assignments, seed-mass classes and fruit fleshiness for 5,201 species, keeping the two classifications separate. It supports comparisons of tree traits and potential dispersal dependencies; it is a selected part of the broader pollination-and-dispersal study, not its complete interaction network. Some species have multiple assignments, and the seed-mass values are class codes rather than measurements in grams.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s42003-026-09896-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-09896-1).

## Tree diversity across the original Amazon forest

**Evidence:** Forest-inventory baseline with spatial interpolation. **Dataset role:** Interpolated tree alpha-diversity baseline, Fisher's alpha..

The study asked how Amazonian tree diversity varies with location and forest type. It found especially high Fisher's alpha in terra-firme forests of western Amazonia's Pebas region and French Guiana. This 0.1-degree grid interpolates that diversity index from old-growth inventories of trees at least 10 cm in diameter at breast height. It provides an ecological baseline across the original lowland forest extent, rather than a map of current habitat condition or restoration effects. Fisher's alpha is dimensionless and differs from species per hectare. Estimates are less certain in sparsely sampled white-sand and swamp forests. Map display awaits CRS confirmation.

Availability: Download only: CRS confirmation needed.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6).

## Amazon tree diversity: plot observations

**Evidence:** Forest inventory observations with spatial modeling in the associated study. **Dataset role:** Plot-level density, richness, diversity and reference data underlying regional models..

Amazon tree diversity varies strongly among forest types and regions, so tree counts or a single basin-wide average cannot describe it adequately. Using 2,046 inventory plots, the study found distinct patterns in density, species richness and Fisher’s alpha diversity, and used forest-type-specific spatial models to map those patterns. This table supplies the plot observations and standardized diversity measures behind that analysis, for free-standing woody stems at least 10 cm in diameter. It supports checking the observational basis of regional biodiversity comparisons. It is not a current forest-condition map or evidence of change through time; the supplied coordinates remain attributes because their datum is unverified.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6).

## Tree species richness across the original Amazon forest

**Evidence:** Forest-inventory baseline with spatial interpolation. **Dataset role:** Interpolated tree species-richness baseline, species per hectare..

Using forest inventories, the study asked where Amazonian tree species richness is greatest and how it varies among forest types. The highest richness was found in terra-firme forests of western Amazonia's Pebas region and French Guiana. This file estimates species per hectare on a 0.1-degree grid, using old-growth plots of trees at least 10 cm in diameter at breast height. It describes the original lowland forest extent, with greater uncertainty in sparsely sampled white-sand and swamp forests. Values are not total species per grid cell or a measure of restoration success. The original grid is downloadable; map display awaits CRS confirmation.

Availability: Download only: CRS confirmation needed.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-023-05514-6).

## ATTO forest carbon exchange during the 2023 Amazon drought

**Evidence:** Local flux observations interpreted alongside regional models. **Dataset role:** 2023 monthly tower flux observations.

During the extreme 2023 drought, measurements near the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory showed that the local forest remained a net carbon sink over the year. The study associated this local response with reduced ecosystem respiration and sufficient soil moisture, while its wider regional analysis found a different carbon balance. This table provides monthly carbon uptake, respiration and net exchange for 2023, supporting investigation of local drought responses and checks on ecosystem models. The linked 2014–2023 source table provides the historical comparison. These tower measurements describe a local forest footprint and cannot establish the carbon balance of the whole Amazon.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1029/2025AV001658](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025AV001658).

## Brazil: additional heat exposure under Amazon savannization

**Evidence:** Climate-model scenario experiment with population exposure analysis. **Dataset role:** Published Figure 3 additional-exposure estimates, with municipal inputs retained as supporting data..

A worst-case climate-model experiment replacing all Amazon rainforest with savanna increased estimated human heat exposure, especially under high emissions. In the late-century RCP8.5 scenario, the study estimated more than 11 million additional people exposed to extreme in-shade heat stress, including about 5.7 million in highly vulnerable communities. These are conditional estimates using 2019 population, not forecasts of population growth or observed health outcomes. This table contains the 12 published Figure 3 estimates across historical and future climates, heat-exposure settings and vulnerability groups. It supports comparing the modeled human consequences of forest loss; the high-vulnerability figures are subsets of the totals and must not be added to them.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s43247-021-00275-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-021-00275-8).

## Chaglla hydropower: irrigation and climate scenarios

**Evidence:** Coupled watershed–reservoir modeling and climate/management scenario analysis. **Dataset role:** Selected modeled mean/firm-power outputs; a subset of the broader multisector analysis..

Upstream irrigation can protect crop production while reducing downstream hydropower in the upper Huallaga basin. Coupled watershed and reservoir models found that irrigation choices could affect Chaglla’s firm power more strongly than climate change alone, making food, energy and ecosystem objectives difficult to satisfy together. This table supplies 336 modeled mean- and firm-power summaries for 16 land-management configurations under selected climate scenarios. It supports inspecting the hydropower side of those comparisons; it is not the full multisector decision analysis or observed electricity generation. Original scenario codes are retained because their mapping and exact period bounds still need confirmation, as does the firm-power reliability criterion.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1038/s43247-024-01738-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-024-01738-4).

## Tropical forest carbon: disturbance losses and recovery

**Evidence:** Literature synthesis and meta-analysis. **Dataset role:** Compiled study estimates underlying disturbance-loss and carbon-recovery comparisons..

Tropical forest degradation can remove substantial aboveground carbon even when forest remains. This synthesis of 146 studies compares losses after different disturbances and carbon stocks during recovery. After 20 years, recovering degraded forests averaged 75% of reference carbon stocks, versus 38% in secondary forests regrowing after clearance. The reference is prior standing or nearby undisturbed forest. This difference includes carbon retained after disturbance; it does not mean faster accumulation. The supplied loss and recovery workbooks contain the compiled study estimates and a reference crosswalk. They support comparisons for carbon accounting and recovery planning, with substantial variation among disturbance types, regions and studies; they are not predictions for a particular site.

Availability: Table/workbook ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1126/sciadv.adz1923](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adz1923).

## Forest water-cycle vulnerability to climate and forest loss

**Evidence:** Diagnostic model using satellite and environmental observations. **Dataset role:** 0.1-degree TFVI diagnostic grid for evapotranspiration..

The study asked how climate stress and forest loss affect tropical forest water and carbon cycling. It identified vulnerability toward lower evapotranspiration in areas of forest loss, including the southern and eastern Amazon, while other tropical areas showed signals toward higher evapotranspiration. This 0.1-degree map is the dimensionless diagnostic index for evapotranspiration, the water returned to the atmosphere by vegetation and evaporation. Negative values indicate vulnerability toward decreases; positive values toward increases, with near-zero values indicating relative stability. It provides context for assessing forest water-cycle pressures. It is not a measurement of water flux, a probability of forest collapse, or an estimate of restoration benefits.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002).

## Tropical forest biomass vulnerability to climate and land-use pressures

**Evidence:** Satellite observations and statistical modeling. **Dataset role:** Derived biomass vulnerability index.

Satellite observations show that tropical forests respond differently to climate and land-use pressures. The study found widespread vulnerability in the Americas and slower recovery of forest carbon cycling as stress increased. This map shows the biomass component of that assessment, helping identify regions for closer monitoring and conservation or restoration planning. Negative index values indicate vulnerability toward biomass loss, positive values toward higher biomass, and values near zero relative stability. It is a standardized diagnostic index based on observed relationships; it does not measure carbon stocks, predict a probability of collapse, or estimate the benefit of a restoration intervention.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002).

## Forest productivity vulnerability to climate and forest loss

**Evidence:** Diagnostic model using satellite and environmental observations. **Dataset role:** 0.1-degree TFVI diagnostic grid for gross primary productivity..

The study asked where climate stress and forest loss are weakening humid tropical forests. It found widespread vulnerability of forest productivity in the Amazon, with stronger signals along the arc of deforestation. This 0.1-degree map is the dimensionless diagnostic index for gross primary productivity. Negative values indicate vulnerability toward lower productivity; positive values toward higher productivity, and values near zero indicate relative stability. It can help identify places for closer assessment alongside land-use and climate evidence. The index describes vulnerability inferred from observed relationships; it does not measure carbon uptake, the probability of forest collapse, or the effect of a restoration intervention.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002).

## Forest surface-warming vulnerability to climate and forest loss

**Evidence:** Diagnostic model using satellite and environmental observations. **Dataset role:** 0.1-degree TFVI diagnostic grid for daytime land-surface temperature..

The study asked how climate stress and forest loss affect tropical forest functioning. Its land-surface temperature analysis found widespread vulnerability toward persistent warming across forests in the Americas, associated with climate warming, atmospheric drying, and tree-cover loss. This 0.1-degree map is the dimensionless diagnostic index for daytime land-surface temperature. Positive values indicate vulnerability toward warming, negative values toward cooling, and values near zero indicate relative stability. The map can help examine where forest cooling functions may be under pressure. Values are not degrees Celsius, the probability of forest collapse, or a prediction of the cooling benefit from planting or restoring forest.

Availability: Map ready.

Paper: [doi:10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oneear.2021.06.002).

## In-shade heat stress: historical climate with complete Amazon savannization

**Evidence:** Climate model scenario experiment. **Dataset role:** Single modeled climate and land-cover scenario (HS), Figure 1..

The study used a climate model to ask how complete replacement of Amazon forest by savanna would change heat stress alongside global warming. Across paired simulations, savannization raised in-shade heat stress, with greater increases under RCP8.5. This file supplies the historical climate (1981-2010) scenario with complete Amazon savannization. Values are degrees Celsius for average daily maximum in-shade wet-bulb globe temperature in the warmest month, on a South American model grid. It is one scenario, not a difference map, observed illness record, or policy-effect estimate. Download is available; map display awaits CRS confirmation.
