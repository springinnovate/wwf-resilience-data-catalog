## Question

How do different land-use and land-cover transitions compare in their ecological consequences, including changes within forests that remain standing?

## What the study found

Using 310 sites, the study compared biodiversity, carbon and soil across seven land-use classes and estimated impacts for 18 transitions. Logged-and-burned versus undisturbed primary forest had 25% lower large-tree richness and 27% lower dung-beetle richness. Aboveground carbon was 28–46% lower in logged or logged-and-burned forest than in undisturbed forest. Combining ecological contrasts with transition rates identified forest conversion to pasture as both prevalent and high impact.

## How to use the evidence

The evidence supports examining degradation and regeneration alongside clearance, and evaluating biodiversity and carbon separately rather than treating forest area as a complete measure of condition. The comparisons are observational across sites, not before-and-after interventions. The delivered table is an input dataset of site aggregates; estimated transition effects, uncertainty and regional transition frequencies require the paper’s analyses and additional inputs.

## How the evidence was obtained

Observational site comparisons combined with land-cover transition analysis

## What this dataset contributes

Site-level biodiversity and carbon inputs underlying comparisons among land-use classes.

## Limits and interpretation

- The field sites are in two eastern Amazon regions and were surveyed during 2010–2011; results should not be read as a causal estimate applicable uniformly across Amazonia.
- Missing values remain NA; orchid bees were sampled only in Paragominas. Forest species means species recorded at least once at a primary-forest site, not necessarily forest specialists.
- Carbon is expressed per hectare, with soil carbon measured at 0–30 cm depth. Coordinates require a verified join and CRS before mapping.
