## Findings

The paper's Discussion reports that 78–83% of sampled Amazonian tree species are animal dispersed, depending on the classification, with arboreal vertebrates prominent. Animal-dispersed species generally have fleshy fruits. A small set of abundant genera contributes a disproportionate share of estimated fruit resources in the broader analysis.

These trait patterns indicate potential dependence of regeneration on animals. They do not measure changes in recruitment after animal loss or the effect of a restoration intervention.

## Using these data

The table contains dispersal classifications and seed traits for 5,201 species. Use it to compare dispersal modes, fruit fleshiness and seed-mass classes, keeping the two classification systems separate. It contains selected species-level traits from the broader network dataset. Conclusions about combined pollination and dispersal dependence or population-weighted networks require fields and calculations beyond this table.

## Limits

- The two classification systems use different categories and some species have multiple assignments; they should not be added together as independent species counts.
- Assignments draw on literature and taxonomic inference, including genus-based assignments where appropriate. They are not all direct observations of interactions for each species.
- The paper gives internally inconsistent exact counts and percentages in its dispersal Results. The summary retains its Discussion's approximate range.
- Seed-mass values are logarithmic class codes, with exact class boundaries unresolved in the delivered metadata.
